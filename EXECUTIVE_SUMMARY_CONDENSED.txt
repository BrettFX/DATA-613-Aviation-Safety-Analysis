EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (CONDENSED)
Aviation Safety Analysis: Uncovering the Risks That Remain
================================================================================

DATA 621 - Data Communication and Visualization | Fall 2025


This comprehensive analysis of 88,889 NTSB aviation accident records spanning 1948-2022 reveals that while accident rates declined 50% since the 1980s peak (from ~2,400 to ~1,100 annually), specific risk patterns persist despite dramatic safety improvements. Using statistical analysis, geospatial mapping, machine learning clustering, and advanced natural language processing, we identified six critical vulnerability categories. Geographic analysis shows accidents concentrate in five states (California, Texas, Florida, Alaska, Arizona) accounting for 34% of incidents, with machine learning identifying 12 distinct risk zones reflecting high flight activity and challenging conditions. Weather acts as a force multiplier: while only 7% of accidents occur in instrument meteorological conditions (IMC), these are 3.6 times more likely to be fatal than visual conditions (57.9% vs. 15.9% fatality rates). Landing and takeoff account for 47% of all accidents, with general aviation piston aircraft representing 78% of incidents, indicating commercial aviation has benefited disproportionately from safety improvements.

Most significantly, AI-powered analysis of 14,000 accident narratives using zero-shot classification (BART-large-MNLI) represents the first large-scale HFACS taxonomy validation on NTSB data. Human factors dominate accident causation with decision errors appearing in 99.5% of analyzed cases, loss of control in 96.9%, and loss of situational awareness in 97.7%. Three distinct error syndromes emerged through hierarchical clustering: Decision Chain Failures (poor decisions plus failure to correct), Perceptual-Cognitive Errors (awareness loss plus spatial disorientation at 67.9% prevalence), and Skill & Training Deficiencies (loss of control linked to inadequate proficiency at 77.6%). Statistical analysis revealed 16 of 21 human factor associations as significant, with strongest correlations between loss of control and situational awareness loss (OR: 3.2). These patterns remain temporally stable across decades despite technological improvements, suggesting fundamental human cognitive limitations rather than equipment failures drive most modern accidents.

Based on these findings, we recommend seven evidence-based interventions prioritized by impact. High-priority actions include human factors-focused training implementing Crew Resource Management principles for general aviation to address the 99.5% decision error prevalence, and enhanced IMC training with mandatory spatial disorientation recognition to reduce the 3.6x fatality multiplier. Medium-priority recommendations include strengthened landing/takeoff procedures (47% of accidents), geographic risk mitigation deploying resources to the 12 identified clusters, and general aviation safety campaigns addressing the 78% accident concentration and 85% procedural violation rate. Ongoing initiatives should include data-driven monitoring using spatial clustering and NLP for pattern detection, and regulatory reforms mandating HFACS-based standardized reporting. These targeted interventions provide a clear roadmap for the next generation of aviation safety improvements, demonstrating that further gains require addressing human performance limitations through evidence-based training reforms and data-driven monitoring systems rather than technology alone.
