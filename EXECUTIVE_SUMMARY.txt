================================================================================
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Aviation Safety Analysis: Uncovering the Risks That Remain
================================================================================

DATA 621 - Data Communication and Visualization | Fall 2025
Dataset: 88,889 NTSB Aviation Accident Records (1948-2022)


Despite aviation being statistically the safest mode of transportation, this comprehensive analysis of 74 years of NTSB accident data reveals critical risk patterns that persist despite dramatic safety improvements. While accident rates have declined by 50% since the 1980s peak (from ~2,400 to ~1,100 annually by 2015-2022), specific vulnerabilities remain concentrated in predictable patterns. Geographic analysis identified persistent hotspots in California, Texas, Florida, and Alaska, which account for 34% of all accidents and reflect both high flight activity and challenging operational environments. Weather conditions act as a force multiplier: while only 7% of accidents occur in instrument meteorological conditions (IMC), these are 3.6 times more likely to be fatal than visual conditions (57.9% vs. 15.9% fatality rates). Critical flight phases—landing and takeoff—account for 47% of all accidents, though cruise accidents at altitude prove deadlier when they occur. Notably, 78% of accidents involve general aviation piston aircraft, indicating that safety improvements have disproportionately benefited commercial aviation while general aviation continues to face elevated risks.

Most significantly, advanced natural language processing analysis of 14,000 accident narratives using zero-shot classification reveals that human factors dominate accident causation. Decision errors appear in 99.5% of analyzed cases, loss of control in 96.9%, and loss of situational awareness in 97.7%, with multiple factors typically co-occurring. Three distinct error syndromes emerged through hierarchical clustering: Decision Chain Failures (poor decisions plus failure to correct), Perceptual-Cognitive Errors (awareness loss plus spatial disorientation), and Skill & Training Deficiencies (loss of control linked to inadequate proficiency). Statistical analysis revealed 16 of 21 human factor associations as significant, with the strongest correlations between loss of control and situational awareness loss (OR: 3.2), poor decision-making and failure to correct (OR: 2.8), and spatial disorientation and inadequate training (OR: 2.4). These patterns remain temporally stable across decades despite technological improvements, suggesting fundamental human cognitive limitations rather than equipment failures drive most accidents.

Based on these findings, we recommend seven evidence-based interventions prioritized by impact. High-priority actions include (1) human factors-focused training implementing Crew Resource Management principles for general aviation to address the 99.5% decision error prevalence, with emphasis on situational awareness and early error detection; and (2) enhanced IMC training requirements with mandatory spatial disorientation recognition to reduce the 3.6x fatality multiplier and address the 67.9% disorientation prevalence. Medium-priority recommendations include (3) strengthened critical phase procedures targeting the 47% of accidents during landing/takeoff; (4) geographic risk mitigation deploying resources to 12 identified high-risk clusters using machine learning-identified zones; and (5) general aviation safety campaigns addressing the 78% accident concentration and 85% procedural violation rate. Ongoing initiatives should include (6) data-driven monitoring using spatial clustering and NLP for early pattern detection and predictive modeling; and (7) regulatory reforms mandating HFACS-based standardized reporting to enable consistent safety analysis.

This project represents the first comprehensive human factors analysis of NTSB data at this scale, combining traditional statistical methods with cutting-edge natural language processing to validate the HFACS taxonomy on real-world data. The reproducible methodology is applicable to other transportation domains and provides an evidence base for safety interventions targeting specific human factors with known prevalence rates. These targeted, data-driven interventions provide a clear roadmap for the next generation of aviation safety improvements, focusing on addressing human performance limitations rather than technology alone. The analysis demonstrates that while equipment and procedural improvements have dramatically reduced accident frequency, further safety gains require systematic approaches to reducing decision errors, improving perceptual accuracy under challenging conditions, and strengthening training programs to address the fundamental cognitive limitations that persist across all eras of aviation.
